## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Religious change unfolds through conviction, patronage, marriage, mobility, coercion, and institutions amid both exchange and violence. |
| **Years** | c. 1000–1700 |
| **Before–after** | From regionally varied encounters to enduring, internally diverse religious communities. |
## Before
- Muslim communities exist through coastal trade before many inland conquests.
- Brahmanical, Buddhist, Jain, devotional, and local traditions already compete and overlap.
## The turn
- Sultanates and regional states alter patronage, law, military service, and settlement.
- Individuals and groups change affiliation for different, often unrecoverable reasons.
## After
- Islamic and Hindu traditions become more regionally rooted and internally differentiated.
- Later census and nationalism recast fluid histories as bounded communities.
## Act I — Many conversions
- Reject a single spectrum from “sword” to “peaceful Sufi.”
- Examine conviction, status, taxation, enslavement, marriage, patronage, and frontier settlement.
- Lack of a source is not evidence for free choice or coercion.
## Act II — Law and inequality
- Jizya, pilgrimage taxes, legal pluralism, and exemptions vary by ruler and period.
- Caste shapes opportunity across confessional boundaries.
- State discrimination and pragmatic accommodation can coexist.
## Act III — Conflict documented
- Chronicle boasts, temple damage, mosque attack, and forced movement require case studies.
- Numbers and motives must be corroborated.
- Avoid denial while refusing inherited collective guilt.
## Act IV — Shared practice
- Languages, music, healing, dress, food, and shrine attendance cross boundaries.
- Borrowing may be unequal and contested.
- “Synthesis” should describe evidence, not romanticize coexistence.
## Act V — Making communities
- Scholars, gurus, ulama, custodians, and rulers police boundaries.
- Polemic can sharpen identity without producing modern nationalism.
- Ordinary households negotiate categories differently from doctrinal texts.
## Subcontinental note
- Regional comparisons prevent Bengal, Punjab, Kerala, Kashmir, or the Deccan becoming a universal model.
- Hinduism and Islam are plural historical formations, never single actors.
## How we know
- Chronicles often turn conversion into royal praise or blame.
- Inscriptions, names, graves, settlements, legal texts, and vernacular memory offer partial indicators.
- Demographic reconstruction is approximate and must state uncertainty.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim all conversions were forced or all were voluntary.
- Do not claim Sufis and states operated as unified agents.
- Do not claim shared custom erased coercion.
- Do not project Partition-era communal nationalism backward.

- Never use generic worship footage as proof of a historical causal claim.
