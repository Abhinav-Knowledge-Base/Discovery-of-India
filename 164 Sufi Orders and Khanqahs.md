## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Sufi lodges and shrines distribute teaching, food, blessing, property, and authority across internally diverse Islamicate worlds. |
| **Years** | c. twelfth–seventeenth centuries |
| **Before–after** | From mobile teachers and early lodges to endowed shrine networks and competing orders. |
## Before
- Muslim merchants, soldiers, scholars, and mystics reach regions by different routes.
- Ascetic and devotional practices already inhabit South Asian landscapes.
## The turn
- Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Shattari, and Naqshbandi lineages establish distinct institutions.
- Khanqahs and dargahs link discipleship to food, land, gifts, and urban publics.
## After
- Hereditary custodians and state patronage reshape charismatic foundations.
- Reformers contest shrine practice from within Muslim traditions.
## Act I — Order and lineage
- Silsila authenticates transmission through a chain of teachers.
- Orders differ over court relations, wealth, music, and discipline.
- “Sufism” is an umbrella, not a single tolerant essence.
## Act II — The khanqah economy
- Lodges require grain, cooks, servants, buildings, and donor support.
- Futuh gifts and endowments complicate ideals of distance from power.
- Hospitality can include broad publics while preserving rank.
## Act III — Court and refusal
- Nizamuddin Auliya's remembered distance from Delhi sultans becomes exemplary.
- Suhrawardi figures often accept closer state relationships.
- Hagiography turns nuanced negotiations into moral anecdotes.
## Act IV — Shrine publics
- Pilgrimage draws Muslims and non-Muslims for vows, healing, music, and status.
- Women participate as patrons and pilgrims despite spatial restrictions.
- Shrine inclusion does not erase conversion pressure, conflict, or doctrinal boundaries.
## Act V — Debate inside Islam
- Naqshbandi criticism targets some court and devotional practices.
- Sama and shrine visitation generate legal disagreement.
- Avoid treating orthodoxy and mysticism as fixed enemies.
## Subcontinental note
- Sufi institutions localize Islam while remaining connected to Arabic, Persian, and Central Asian scholarship.
- Shared shrine use is historical practice, not proof of timeless communal harmony.
## How we know
- Malfuzat record remembered conversations edited by disciples.
- Tazkiras and miracle tales establish lineage and must be read institutionally.
- Endowment deeds, inscriptions, architecture, graves, and music offer material checks.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim Sufis converted India peacefully as a single mission.
- Do not claim all orders rejected rulers.
- Do not claim shared pilgrimage dissolved religious difference.
- Do not claim hagiographic miracles are direct event records.
