## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | A displaced Timurid prince conquers Delhi and Agra, then struggles to turn battlefield success into a solvent dynasty. |
| **Years** | 1483–1530 |
| **Before–after** | From loss of Samarkand to a fragile Indo-Timurid realm. |
## Before
- Babur inherits prestige without a secure Central Asian base.
- Lodi authority is weakened by noble conflict, not by inevitable decline.
## The turn
- Kabul supplies Babur a base for repeated incursions into Punjab.
- Panipat, Khanwa, and Ghaghra defeat distinct coalitions.
## After
- Babur claims Hindustan but governs through inherited officials and uncertain loyalties.
- Humayun receives a treasury and army strained by conquest.
## Act I — Timurid inheritance
- Chinggisid connection, Timur's memory, and appanage politics frame Babur's claims.
- His early defeats matter to the identity crafted in his memoir.
- “Mughal” conceals his Chagatai-Timurid self-understanding.
## Act II — Kabul to Punjab
- Control of mountain routes, horses, and gardens sustains a mobile court.
- Punjab raids supply resources and intelligence.
- Local invitations serve factional interests rather than national welcome.
## Act III — Panipat
- Field fortification, artillery, cavalry, and Lodi disunity combine in victory.
- Babur's account magnifies order against enemy mass.
- Technology supports, but does not predetermine, conquest.
## Act IV — Khanwa and Ghaghra
- Rana Sanga's coalition is politically diverse and formidable.
- Babur deploys Islamic rhetoric alongside Timurid legitimacy and material rewards.
- Afghan resistance persists after Ibrahim's death.
## Act V — Governing Hindustan
- Treasury distribution builds loyalty while creating fiscal pressure.
- Gardens and surveys translate conquest into ordered kingship.
- Babur dies before institutions secure succession.
## Subcontinental note
- Babur enters a multipolar landscape whose actors retain agency.
- Victory extends claims faster than administrative control.
## How we know
- The *Baburnama* combines observation, omission, confession, and self-fashioning.
- Afghan, Rajput, Persian, numismatic, and battlefield evidence complicate it.
- Later illustrated manuscripts show Akbar's Babur as much as Babur's world.
## Do not claim
- Do not call Babur simply a Mongol invader.
- Do not treat his memoir as neutral reportage.
- Do not reduce Khanwa to Hindu versus Muslim.
- Do not equate victory with uniform Mughal control.
