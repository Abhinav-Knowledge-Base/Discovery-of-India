## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |

| **Logline** | Rajput houses use marriage, mansab, rebellion, and memory to negotiate Mughal expansion on unequal terms. |
| **Years** | c. 1550–1707 |
| **Before–after** | From competing regional houses to deep but contested participation in empire. |
## Before
- “Rajput” encompasses houses with different genealogies, resources, and rivals.
- Mewar, Marwar, Amber, Bundi, and others pursue distinct strategies.
## The turn
- Akbar's alliance with Amber creates marriage and service precedents.
- Chittor's fall and Mewar's resistance demonstrate coercive alternatives.
## After
- Rajput commanders become central to Mughal war and governance.
- Aurangzeb's Marwar succession intervention provokes prolonged rebellion.
## Act I — Making Rajput status
- Genealogies, bardic traditions, and marriage regulate clan rank.
- Warrior identity consolidates historically rather than descending unchanged from antiquity.
- Peasants, Bhils, artisans, and women sustain fortress states.
## Act II — Amber at court
- Bharmal's alliance advances Kachhwaha survival and opportunity.
- Man Singh commands from Bengal to Kabul while patronizing temples.
- Imperial service permits agency within hierarchy, not equality between states.
## Act III — Mewar resists
- Chittor's siege includes mass killing and jauhar remembered through later politics.
- Maharana Pratap rebuilds authority from difficult terrain.
- Haldighati is neither final defeat nor national war.
## Act IV — Women and marriage
- Imperial marriages create kinship, rank, wealth, and constrained female mobility.
- Mughal sources often omit women's personal names.
- Rajput queens exercise patronage and mediation despite archival silence.
## Act V — Marwar crisis
- Jaswant Singh's death triggers dispute over succession and imperial custody.
- Durgadas Rathore sustains resistance around the young Ajit Singh.
- Religious policy matters alongside sovereignty, office, and fiscal pressure.
## Subcontinental note
- Alliance and resistance coexist within houses and across generations.
- Rajput politics cannot be reduced to Hindu response to Muslim rule.
## How we know
- Mughal chronicles, khyats, bardic poetry, inscriptions, paintings, and forts disagree productively.
- Genealogies legitimize present claims by reshaping past kinship.
- Later nationalist memorials require separation from sixteenth-century evidence.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim all Rajputs chose one imperial strategy.
- Do not romanticize jauhar or erase its coercive gender setting.
- Do not call Haldighati a Hindu–Muslim national battle.
- Do not treat service as either simple collaboration or equality.
