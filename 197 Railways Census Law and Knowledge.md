## Before

Roads, rivers, pilgrimage, merchant networks, customary law, and scholarly traditions already moved people and organized knowledge.

## The turn

Colonial institutions standardized gauges, categories, codes, maps, and credentials for fiscal, military, commercial, and governing ends.

## After

Indians appropriated trains, courts, statistics, and education, even as these systems hardened identities and distributed benefits unequally.

## Act I - Railway political economy

Guarantees protected British investors while Indian taxes, labor, land, and passengers sustained the network.

## Act II - Census categories

Enumerators negotiated language, caste, tribe, religion, gender, and occupation with households and local elites.

## Act III - Codes and courts

Written law promised consistency but translated custom through judges, experts, and unequal access.

## Act IV - Survey and science

Maps, archaeology, botany, and medicine gathered knowledge useful to control and scholarship.

## Act V - Unintended publics

Stations, print, petitions, and associations enabled mobility, debate, organizing, and anti-colonial politics.

## Subcontinental note

Rail and knowledge networks crossed later borders into present Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Myanmar; frontier regions were mapped and governed differently.

## How we know

Timetables, census schedules, court files, maps, engineering drawings, textbooks, photographs, and oral histories show systems in practice.

## Do not claim

- Railways were a simple gift of modernization.
- Census merely discovered fixed communities.
- Codified law treated everyone equally.
- Colonial science contained no Indian expertise.
