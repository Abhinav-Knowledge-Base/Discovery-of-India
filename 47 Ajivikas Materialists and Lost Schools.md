## Before
Begin before the famous names: Ajivikas, materialists, skeptics, and lost schools made early Indian philosophy a crowded argument. Map older settlements, routes, ecologies, and communities without projecting later borders backward.

## The turn
Show how Ajivikas, materialists, skeptics, and lost schools made early Indian philosophy a crowded argument. Ask who provided food, craft skill, transport, credit, ritual work, and military labor—and who paid the human and environmental costs.

## After
Political change redirected patronage and routes but did not reset everyday life. Follow successor institutions, local adaptations, and the later memories that made this episode look more inevitable than it was.

## Act I — Landscape and inheritance
Open across Barabar, Rajgir, Shravasti. Rivers, soils, forests, passes, ports, and seasonal movement constrain choices without mechanically causing political outcomes.

## Act II — Power in practice
Move between courts and non-court institutions. Treat sovereignty as negotiated among rulers, councils, officials, lineages, monasteries, guilds, villages, and frontier brokers rather than uniformly centralized.

## Act III — Work, gender, and exchange
Shift to cultivators, herders, artisans, transport workers, merchants, dependent labor, women donors, and religious specialists. Elite silence is not historical absence; identify what the camera can and cannot recover.

## Act IV — Sources disagree
Set witnesses against one another: hostile textual reports, Barabar inscriptions, cave archaeology, and careful inference. Separate a text's composition and redaction dates from the older world it may represent, and treat Buddhist and Jain traditions critically rather than dismissively.

## Act V — Contingent legacies
Close without a civilizational handoff. Track regional alternatives, environmental limits, maritime and frontier lanes, and the later political or religious uses of Ajivikas Materialists and Lost Schools.

## Subcontinental note
Keep the Ganga plain, northwest, Deccan, far south, Sri Lanka, forests, and sea routes on parallel clocks. Imperial silence never proves political emptiness; southern and frontier polities remain actors in their own histories.

## How we know
The evidentiary spine is hostile textual reports, Barabar inscriptions, cave archaeology, and careful inference. Contemporary inscriptions generally outrank later legend, but their formulas, placement, damage, patronage, intended audience, and modern discovery still demand interpretation.

## Do not claim
- Do not claim that **Ajivikas Materialists and Lost Schools** names one uniform culture, population, or inevitable stage of national unity.
- Do not claim that later stories about **Makkhali Gosala** are eyewitness records unless their date and transmission support it.
- Do not claim that the institutions behind **Ajivikas Materialists and Lost Schools** exercised complete, uniform control across every mapped locality.
- Do not claim that elite or monastic sources exhaust women's, workers', forest peoples', pastoralists', traders', or enslaved people's experience in c. sixth–third centuries BCE.
