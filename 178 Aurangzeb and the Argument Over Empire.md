## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Aurangzeb's half-century reign expands Mughal claims while fiscal strain, religious policy, war, and regional resistance expose imperial limits. |
| **Years** | 1658–1707 |
| **Before–after** | From succession-war victory to vast territorial claims and an overextended Deccan state. |
## Before
- Shah Jahan's sons command armies and provincial networks before open war.
- Mughal finances are strong but depend on continued agrarian collection.
## The turn
- Aurangzeb defeats Dara, Shuja, and Murad, then confines Shah Jahan.
- Bijapur and Golconda annexations commit the court to protracted Deccan war.
## After
- The emperor dies with succession unresolved and armies dispersed.
- Regional states appropriate Mughal practices rather than merely escaping collapse.
## Act I — Seizing sovereignty
- Samugarh and subsequent campaigns decide succession by force.
- Dara's execution uses political and religious accusations.
- Coronation rhetoric cannot substitute for coalition analysis.
## Act II — Law, piety, and patronage
- The *Fatawa-i Alamgiri* compiles Hanafi jurisprudence for learned use.
- Jizya returns in 1679 with exemptions, evasion, and symbolic force.
- Aurangzeb grants some temples and destroys or restricts others in specific political contexts.
## Act III — Revenue and rebellion
- Jat, Satnami, Sikh, Rajput, and other conflicts have distinct fiscal, local, and religious causes.
- Guru Tegh Bahadur's execution requires Sikh and Mughal evidence.
- No single policy explains all resistance.
## Act IV — Deccan war
- Bijapur and Golconda fall, but annexation multiplies garrisons and assignments.
- Maratha forces exploit terrain, forts, intelligence, and revenue claims.
- Mughal presence remains formidable without becoming uniform control.
## Act V — Evidence and argument
- Court chroniclers, orders, temple records, letters, and regional sources conflict.
- Later colonial and nationalist narratives select evidence polemically.
- End with costs borne by soldiers, peasants, towns, and mobile court labor.
## Subcontinental note
- Aurangzeb was neither only a pious villain nor merely a pragmatic administrator.
- Fiscal, military, religious, and regional evidence must remain in the same frame.
## How we know
- *Alamgirnama*, *Maasir-i Alamgiri*, news reports, and letters have distinct purposes.
- Farmans and temple documents prove particular acts, not universal policy.
- Revenue data, forts, coinage, and regional chronicles expose administrative limits.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim Aurangzeb destroyed all temples.
- Do not claim religious policy was irrelevant to his reign.
- Do not explain Mughal strain through religion alone.
- Do not equate maximum map extent with effective uniform control.
