## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Afghan, Rajput, and Timurid contenders struggle over North India until Panipat opens a new, still-fragile imperial claim. |
| **Years** | 1451–1527 |
| **Before–after** | From Lodi restoration of Delhi power to Babur's victories at Panipat and Khanwa. |
## Before
- Sayyid weakness leaves Delhi among several competing fifteenth-century centers.
- Afghan chiefs and Rajput houses hold power through layered kinship and service.
## The turn
- Bahlul Lodi takes Delhi in 1451 and rebuilds authority through Afghan alliances.
- Ibrahim Lodi's centralizing conflict invites defections and Babur's intervention.
## After
- Babur defeats Ibrahim at Panipat in 1526 using field tactics and gunpowder.
- Khanwa checks Rana Sanga's coalition but does not settle the subcontinent.
## Act I — Afghan kingship
- Bahlul negotiates with chiefs rather than commanding a bureaucratic machine.
- Sikandar expands toward Jaunpur and founds Agra.
- Lodi tombs materialize elite memory beyond palace chronicles.
## Act II — Rajput powers
- Mewar under Rana Sanga builds a coalition through war and marriage.
- “Rajput” identity is consolidating, internally ranked, and politically diverse.
- Rajput rulers ally with and fight Muslim rulers across shifting contests.
## Act III — Ibrahim's crisis
- Afghan nobles resist Ibrahim's efforts to reorder status and office.
- Daulat Khan Lodi and Alam Khan seek Babur's aid for their own ends.
- Invitation does not make conquest inevitable or uncontested.
## Act IV — Panipat
- Babur deploys carts, matchlocks, cannon, cavalry, and disciplined formations.
- Ottoman-linked expertise matters, but technology alone does not explain victory.
- The battle kills Ibrahim and many soldiers; casualty figures remain uncertain.
## Act V — Khanwa
- Rana Sanga's coalition threatens Babur's fragile position in 1527.
- Babur frames victory through jihad rhetoric alongside Timurid statecraft and reward.
- Religious language mattered without turning every participant into a communal bloc.
## Subcontinental note
- North India was multipolar; “foreign invader versus native resistance” falsifies mixed coalitions.
- Mughal reach begins as a claim dependent on forts, revenue, and defections.
## How we know
- Babur's memoir is vivid, self-fashioning, and incomplete.
- Afghan and Rajput traditions often survive in later chronicles and bardic texts.
- Coins, tombs, forts, weapons, and battlefield topography test narrative claims.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim Lodis represented all Afghans as one faction.
- Do not claim Rajputs formed one national resistance.
- Do not claim gunpowder made Babur's victory automatic.
- Do not claim Khanwa gave Babur uniform control of India.
