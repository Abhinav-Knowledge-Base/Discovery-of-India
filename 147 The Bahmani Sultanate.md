## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | A rebel Deccan sultanate builds institutions across Gulbarga and Bidar while faction, frontier war, and local society reshape it. |
| **Years** | 1347–1527 |
| **Before–after** | From revolt against Delhi to five successor sultanates. |
## Before
- Delhi's Deccan regime depends on mobile commanders and contested provincial authority.
- Marathi, Kannada, Telugu, Persianate, and older landed networks overlap.
## The turn
- Ala al-Din Hasan Bahman Shah is proclaimed sultan in 1347.
- Gulbarga becomes a capital for an independent Deccan political project.
## After
- Bidar's court develops a distinct Persianate-Deccani culture amid factional struggle.
- Provincial governors convert weakening central power into successor states.
## Act I — Revolt becomes dynasty
- Separate contemporary evidence from legends linking Hasan to a Brahman patron.
- Explain titles, coinage, khutba, and appointment as claims to sovereignty.
- Local alliances matter as much as imported models.
## Act II — Raichur frontier
- Bahmani and Vijayanagara armies repeatedly contest forts and fertile doabs.
- Raids, captives, tribute, and negotiation burden frontier communities.
- Rivalry crosses religious categories through mercenaries and diplomacy.
## Act III — Building a Deccan court
- Gulbarga mosque and tombs adapt regional craft to transregional forms.
- The capital moves to Bidar under Ahmad Shah Wali.
- Sufi affiliation supports legitimacy but does not make politics uniformly tolerant.
## Act IV — Faction and administration
- “Deccani” and “Afaqi” name shifting coalitions, not immutable ethnic camps.
- Mahmud Gawan's madrasa and reforms embody opportunity and conflict.
- His execution in 1481 exposes court intrigue and royal vulnerability.
## Act V — Fragmentation
- Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Berar, Bidar, and Golconda emerge unevenly.
- Dynastic breakup does not end shared diplomatic and cultural circuits.
- The Bahmani legacy remains institutionally alive in successor courts.
## Subcontinental note
- Deccan sovereignty connects Delhi precedents, Iranian migration, African military careers, and local elites.
- Neither Islam nor regional identity forms one political interest.
## How we know
- Firishta writes later and must be checked against inscriptions, coins, and buildings.
- Epigraphy records patronage while obscuring coercion and failed commands.
- Forts, ceramics, and waterworks place court narratives in material landscapes.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim Hasan's legendary ancestry is certain.
- Do not claim Deccani–Afaqi rivalry was simply racial or sectarian.
- Do not claim Raichur was a permanent religious frontier.
- Do not claim administrative reform meant uniform control.
