## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Tamil poet-saints become temple canons through centuries of recitation, royal patronage, caste negotiation, and institutional memory. |
| **Years** | c. sixth–twelfth centuries, with later afterlives |
| **Before–after** | From mobile devotional voices to organized Shaiva and Vaishnava temple traditions. |
## Before
- Tamil landscapes already hold Jain, Buddhist, Brahmanical, and local cult institutions.
- Early poet-saint chronology remains approximate and retrospectively ordered.
## The turn
- Alvar and Nayanar poems make loving address to Vishnu and Shiva publicly memorable.
- Chola-era collection and temple liturgy give selected voices canonical form.
## After
- Hagiographies organize diverse saints into bounded communities.
- Sri Vaishnava and Shaiva Siddhanta institutions develop distinct theologies.
## Act I — Voices and places
- Poems name specific shrines, bodies, emotions, and landscapes.
- Oral performance complicates any search for a single original text.
- Vernacular devotion complements and contests Sanskrit authority.
## Act II — Making canons
- Nathamuni is credited with recovering the *Nalayira Divya Prabandham*.
- Nambiyandar Nambi and Chola patronage shape the *Tevaram* corpus.
- Recovery legends express institutional authority rather than stenographic history.
## Act III — Temple machinery
- Land grants support priests, singers, dancers, cooks, guards, and festivals.
- Bronze processions carry deities and saint images into public space.
- Patronage binds devotion to taxation and hierarchy.
## Act IV — Caste and gender
- Nandanar, Tiruppan Alvar, Andal, and Karaikkal Ammaiyar expose unequal access.
- Hagiography may honor marginalized saints while leaving institutions stratified.
- Women's voices survive through male transmission and contested authorship.
## Act V — Polemic and plurality
- Some hymns attack Jain and Buddhist rivals in contexts of patronage competition.
- Do not turn theological polemic into timeless communal hatred.
- End with living recitation and debate over who may enter and interpret.
## Subcontinental note
- Tamil bhakti influences wider devotional worlds without being their single origin.
- Shaiva and Vaishnava traditions are internally diverse, not monolithic “Hinduism.”
## How we know
- Poems are layered literary evidence, not simple biographies.
- Inscriptions document singers, gifts, and temple personnel.
- Bronzes, architecture, hagiography, and performance reveal institutional afterlives.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim bhakti automatically abolished caste.
- Do not claim every saint's traditional dates are secure.
- Do not claim devotional inclusion meant equal temple access.
- Do not claim Jain–Shaiva conflict was permanent or uniform.
