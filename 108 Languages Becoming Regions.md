# Ep 108 — Languages Becoming Regions

[Series README](README.md)

## Treatment
| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Inscriptions, poems, courts, monasteries, markets, and oral performers make languages newly public, helping regions form without fixing modern nations. |
| **Years** | c. 500–1200 CE |
| **Before–after** | Sanskrit, Prakrit, Tamil, and oral multilingual worlds → expanding Kannada, Telugu, Malayalam, Bengali-Assamese, Odia, Marathi, and other textual cultures |

## Before
People already spoke many languages; survival in writing depended on scripts, materials, institutions, prestige, and chance.

## The turn
Courts and religious communities increasingly authorize regional languages for inscription, poetics, devotion, genealogy, and administration.

## After
Literary regions gain durable canons, but boundaries, standards, scripts, and identities continue changing far beyond 1200.

## Act I — Speech before evidence
Explain why a first surviving inscription is not a language’s birthday and why oral histories leave few datable objects.

## Act II — Cosmopolitan Sanskrit
Present Sanskrit as mobile and prestigious but locally remade, never the sole language of thought or power.

## Act III — Kannada, Telugu, Malayalam
Compare Halmidi, *Kavirajamarga*, Nannaya, and early Malayalam records while resisting teleological state-language maps.

## Act IV — Eastern continuums
Explore early Bengali-Assamese, Odia, Maithili, and related forms as overlapping developments whose labels are often retrospective.

## Act V — Who enters a canon?
Center Jain, Buddhist, Shaiva, Vaishnava, courtly, women’s, artisanal, Dalit, Adivasi, and oral voices, including those excluded by manuscript preservation.

## Subcontinental note
Sinhala, Tibetan, Newar, Persian-Arabic Sindhi, and northeastern language histories meet South Asian networks without being stages in one Indian linguistic destiny.

## How we know
Inscriptions, paleography, manuscripts copied later, grammatical works, loanwords, oral forms, and comparative linguistics yield ranges rather than clean birth dates.

## Do not claim
- Do not give any language a single birth year.
- Do not map modern linguistic states onto the early medieval period.
- Do not call Sanskrit simply dead, foreign, or universally spoken.
- Do not equate script, language, ethnicity, and political loyalty.
- Do not let surviving elite texts erase oral, caste, gender, and forest-community speech.

## Figures, sites, and works
| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Halmidi inscription | Early substantial Kannada record with dating debates |
| *Kavirajamarga* | Kannada poetics and a mapped literary region |
| Nannaya | Foundational but not “first speaker” of Telugu |
| Vazhappally inscription | Early Malayalam-region record in Vatteluttu |
| Charyapada | Buddhist songs claimed by several eastern language histories |
| Sarala-era precursors | Reminder that later Odia canon rests on earlier inscriptional growth |

## Locations and visual evidence
Film inscriptions in changing scripts, palm-leaf preparation, recitation, marketplaces, border dialect continua, and digital manuscript comparison; color maps with gradients.
