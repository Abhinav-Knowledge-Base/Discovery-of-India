# Ep 107 — Guilds, Ports, and Oceanic Commerce

[Series README](README.md)

## Treatment
| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Merchant bodies, ship crews, artisans, rulers, and port communities connect inland production to an ocean no dynasty controls. |
| **Years** | c. 500–1200 CE |
| **Before–after** | Post-Roman trade transformations → expansive Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea networks |

## Before
Indian Ocean exchange long predated European arrival, linking seasonal winds, coastal pilots, inland routes, credit, pilgrimage, and migration.

## The turn
Ayyavole, Manigramam, Anjuvannam, Nanadesi, and other labels appear across inscriptions as merchants negotiate privileges and protection.

## After
By 1200, commercial networks remain dynamic amid new states and Muslim, Jewish, Christian, Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain diasporas.

## Act I — Follow one cargo
Trace pepper or textiles from cultivator and artisan through carriers, markets, customs points, financiers, ships, and distant consumers.

## Act II — What was a guild?
Compare inscriptional bodies across places; avoid making every name a modern corporation with fixed membership and headquarters.

## Act III — Port cities move
Use sediment, river shifts, storms, and archaeology to explain why Muziris, Mantai, Sopara, Tamralipti, and others changed.

## Act IV — People aboard
Center sailors, dockworkers, women traders, brokers, enslaved people, migrants, and religious communities alongside elite donors.

## Act V — Oceanic plurality
Show Arabic, Persian, Tamil, Kannada, Malayalam, Sinhala, Southeast Asian, and Chinese actors co-producing commerce without a single master civilization.

## Subcontinental note
Sri Lanka and Southeast Asian, Arabian, East African, and Chinese ports appear as partners with their own histories, never as extensions of an Indian maritime empire.

## How we know
Merchant inscriptions, shipwrecks, ceramics, coins, harbor archaeology, foreign geographies, and literary texts measure different parts of trade and leave volume uncertain.

## Do not claim
- Do not call every merchant association a centralized guild.
- Do not infer trade volume directly from one coin or ceramic find.
- Do not describe “Indianization” as one-way colonization of Southeast Asia.
- Do not claim rulers controlled all oceanic commerce.
- Do not erase coerced labor, piracy, environmental risk, or small traders.

## Figures, sites, and works
| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Ayyavole Five Hundred | Widely attested merchant identity with local variation |
| Manigramam | Merchant body named in South and Southeast Asian records |
| Anjuvannam | Commercial community label with debated composition |
| Barus / Lobu Tua inscription | Tamil merchant presence in Sumatra |
| Mantai | Sri Lankan entrepôt linking multiple seas |
| Belitung shipwreck | Ninth-century cargo showing Asian maritime scale |

## Locations and visual evidence
Film port sediments, ceramic scatters, warehouses, boatbuilding, monsoon sailing, inland looms and pepper gardens; map dated finds, not imagined national fleets.
