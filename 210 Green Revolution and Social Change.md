## Before

Food deficits, drought, unequal landholding, regional research, and dependence on grain imports shaped agricultural policy.

## The turn

State support and farmer adoption concentrated high-yield wheat and rice in irrigated districts before spreading unevenly.

## After

Greater food availability reduced import dependence, but class, caste, gender, ecological, and regional consequences remain contested.

## Act I - A package, not a seed

Credit, electricity, canals, fertilizer, extension, prices, and procurement made new varieties viable.

## Act II - Regional concentration

Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh advanced first; rainfed and eastern regions received fewer benefits.

## Act III - Labor and caste

Mechanization, wages, migration, tenancy, and land values changed bargaining for Dalit laborers and smallholders.

## Act IV - Gendered work

Women's seed knowledge and labor remained essential while titles, credit, machines, and extension often favored men.

## Act V - Ecological ledger

Groundwater depletion, salinity, pesticide exposure, stubble, and reduced diversity accumulated beside food gains.

## Subcontinental note

Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, later rice regions, dryland India, and Pakistan's Punjab show varied adoption; comparisons prevent one national verdict.

## How we know

Crop statistics, farm budgets, procurement records, oral histories, health studies, groundwater data, photographs, and seed collections measure different outcomes.

## Do not claim

- One scientist or seed caused the Green Revolution.
- All farmers benefited equally.
- Higher yields prove ecological sustainability.
- Traditional farming was static and uniformly benign.
