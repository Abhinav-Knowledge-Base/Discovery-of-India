## Act 1 — The crowded field
Before the episode's turn, authority in this region was divided among courts, local chiefs, corporate bodies, and communities managing land, water, labor, and routes. Hoysalas, Kakatiyas, Seunas, Sri Lankan kingdoms, and Delhi pursued separate agendas.

## Act 2 — The historical turn
Jatavarman Sundara Pandya converted campaigns, pearl-port revenue, and temple gifts into paramount claims before succession war opened the south to intervention. The film distinguishes a raid for movable wealth, conquest in battle, annexation through continuing rule, temple patronage, and temple plunder.

## Act 3 — Material power
Pandya inscriptions, coins, Srirangam gifts, Kayal archaeology, pearl banks, and travel accounts show how sovereignty depended on artisans, accountants, transport, food, animals, water, and maintained infrastructure.

## Act 4 — Lives inside the claim
Follow peasants facing dues and ecological risk; artisans and merchants negotiating demand; women exercising constrained agency; enslaved people and captives enduring coercion; and forest, pastoral, coastal, or frontier communities shaping routes.

## Act 5 — Limits and afterlives
Afterward, the new settlement remained uneven: military success had to become tribute, garrisons, appointments, revenue, patronage, or negotiated local compliance. Later memory selected heroes and disasters that contemporary evidence does not always support.

## Subcontinental note
Hoysalas, Kakatiyas, Seunas, Sri Lankan kingdoms, and Delhi pursued separate agendas. Political simultaneity matters: no dynasty occupied the whole subcontinent, and developments elsewhere were not merely reactions to Delhi or the featured court.

## How we know
Pandya inscriptions, coins, Srirangam gifts, Kayal archaeology, pearl banks, and travel accounts must be cross-read because inscriptions praise donors, chronicles shape moral narratives, monuments change over time, and archaeology preserves unevenly. Absence from elite records is not evidence that poorer, mobile, female, or enslaved people were absent.

## Do not claim
- Marco Polo is a situated traveler, not complete eyewitness ethnography.
- A victory inscription or court chronicle proves uniform, permanent control over every place it names.
- Raid, conquest, annexation, temple patronage, and temple plunder were interchangeable acts.
- Monumental patronage reveals the beliefs or benefits of every subject.
- The conflict can be reduced to an inevitable Hindu-versus-Muslim civilizational struggle.

Show landscape constraints—river shift, monsoon, forest, dry zone, pass, pasture, or frontier—where they explain historical choices.
