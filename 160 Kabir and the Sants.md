## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Kabir's abrasive verses move through workshops, sects, manuscripts, and song, refusing neat Hindu–Muslim ownership. |
| **Years** | c. fifteenth–seventeenth centuries of formation |
| **Before–after** | From a poorly documented poet milieu to several institutional Kabir canons. |
## Before
- North Indian towns host weavers, Nath yogis, Vaishnavas, Sufis, and occupational groups.
- “Sant” is a retrospective family resemblance, not one centralized movement.
## The turn
- Verses attributed to Kabir attack ritual pride, caste, scripture, and sectarian certainty.
- Oral circulation makes a voice larger than any recoverable biography.
## After
- Sikh, Dadupanthi, Kabirpanthi, and regional collections preserve different Kabirs.
- Monasteries, gurus, and property turn anti-institutional speech into institutions.
## Act I — Searching for Kabir
- Banaras and Maghar anchor rival but meaningful memory landscapes.
- Weaver identity situates labor without proving every biographical detail.
- Avoid choosing hagiography as straightforward fact.
## Act II — The spoken blow
- Sakhis and pads use paradox, insult, domestic image, and bodily metaphor.
- Nirgun devotion contests fixed divine form while retaining diverse vocabularies.
- “Ram” cannot automatically be equated with one later political meaning.
## Act III — Manuscript Kabirs
- The *Bijak*, *Adi Granth*, and Rajasthani traditions differ significantly.
- Variation is evidence of communities, not merely textual corruption.
- Performance continues to remake emphasis.
## Act IV — Caste and gender
- Kabir condemns birth pride, yet communities transmitting him remain caste-located.
- Masculine renunciant language can marginalize women's experiences.
- Workshops and households support devotional production.
## Act V — Sect and public memory
- Kabirpanths create initiation, lineage, sites, manuscripts, and authority.
- Modern reformers and nationalists select different “universal” Kabirs.
- Keep his irreducible critique alongside institutional history.
## Subcontinental note
- Shared vocabulary shows encounter, not a frictionless religious synthesis.
- Sectarian distinctions were real but were not modern communal nationalism.
## How we know
- No secure authorial manuscript survives from Kabir's lifetime.
- Recensions, linguistic layers, hagiographies, and song performance require comparison.
- Material sites primarily document later remembrance.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim Kabir founded a modern interfaith movement.
- Do not claim all attributed poems are equally early.
- Do not claim caste critique abolished caste practice.
- Do not assign Kabir exclusively to Hinduism or Islam.
