## Before
- Both regions possess independent sultanate institutions and powerful merchant networks.
- Portuguese fortifications constrain some sea routes without controlling all trade.
## The turn
- Akbar takes Gujarat and Bengal through campaigns followed by recurrent rebellion.
- Surat, Cambay, Hooghly, and Dhaka connect imperial revenue to oceanic exchange.
## After
- European companies gain privileges while remaining dependent on Indian states and brokers.
- Coastal wealth fuels regional actors beyond direct court supervision.
## Act I — Conquest is not settlement
- Gujarat rebels after Akbar's first rapid victory.
- Bengal's rivers shelter Afghan chiefs and local zamindars.
- Provincial administration advances through forts, alliances, and repeated force.
## Act II — Surat and pilgrimage
- Surat becomes a customs center and embarkation port for the hajj.
- Bohra, Armenian, Jain, Hindu, Muslim, and European merchants pursue distinct interests.
- Imperial officials bargain with merchant capital.
## Act III — Bengal's river economy
- Rice, sugar, silk, saltpeter, and textiles flow through dense waterways.
- Dhaka expands with provincial government and textile production.
- Weaver prosperity and coercive advances vary by place and period.
## Act IV — Companies at the edge
- Portuguese, Dutch, English, and French companies seek farmans and fortified trade.
- Their ships offer force but do not create immediate colonial sovereignty.
- Asian merchants remain major carriers and financiers.
## Act V — Limits at sea
- Mughals lack a permanent blue-water navy but use ships, forts, and allies.
- The 1632 Hooghly assault targets Portuguese power, captives, and unauthorized fortification.
- Coastal command remains layered among court, province, port, and community.
## Subcontinental note
- The Mughal Empire is part of an Indian Ocean economy, not a sealed land empire.
- Imperial reach at ports differs from control at sea or in weaving villages.
## How we know
- Factory records are detailed but commercially biased.
- Mughal farmans, customs accounts, merchant letters, coins, textiles, and port archaeology diversify evidence.
- Export totals are estimates, not direct measures of social welfare.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim Europeans dominated seventeenth-century Indian trade.
- Do not claim Mughal conquest pacified Bengal immediately.
- Do not treat merchants as one community.
- Do not equate export growth with universal prosperity.
