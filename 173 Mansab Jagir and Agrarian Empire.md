## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Ranks, revenue assignments, surveys, and village negotiations turn harvests into imperial armies—unevenly and often violently. |
| **Years** | c. 1570–1707 |
| **Before–after** | From conquest coalition to a vast fiscal-military system under mounting strain. |
## Before
- Sultanate and Sur regimes already assign revenue and classify officials.
- Peasants, zamindars, village bodies, and moneylenders mediate state demands.
## The turn
- Akbar's court regularizes mansab ranks and transferable jagir assignments.
- Todar Mal-associated assessment systems quantify cultivated land and prices.
## After
- Expansion multiplies officers faster than desirable assignments.
- Eighteenth-century successor states inherit and alter Mughal fiscal practices.
## Act I — The ranked household
- Mansab specifies status and expected cavalry obligation, with revisions over time.
- Branding and muster seek to check fraud.
- Rank depends on patronage, service, and court proximity.
## Act II — The jagir
- Jagirdars collect assigned revenue but do not usually own the soil.
- Transfer limits local entrenchment while encouraging short-term extraction.
- Madad-i maash grants support scholars and religious institutions.
## Act III — Measuring revenue
- Zabt uses measured area, crop rates, and decennial prices in suitable regions.
- Other areas use tribute, crop sharing, or negotiated sums.
- The *Ain-i Akbari* describes norms, not universal practice.
## Act IV — Village power
- Zamindars range from local headmen to armed regional chiefs.
- Cultivators differentiate by land, caste, crop, gender, and credit.
- Resistance includes flight, concealment, petition, and rebellion.
## Act V — Strain and adaptation
- Deccan war, rising rank numbers, and assignment shortage sharpen competition.
- “Jagirdari crisis” is a debated model, not a single collapse switch.
- Revenue machinery survives through regional adaptation.
## Subcontinental note
- Mughal power reaches villages through intermediaries and never becomes uniformly bureaucratic.
- Agrarian growth and coercive extraction can occur together.
## How we know
- Administrative manuals privilege official ideals and aggregate estimates.
- Farmans, account books, petitions, European reports, and village documents show variation.
- Archaeobotany, settlement, coin hoards, and irrigation evidence test economic claims.
## Do not claim
- Do not equate jagir with private landed property.
- Do not claim zabt covered the whole empire.
- Do not treat peasants as an undifferentiated class.
- Do not present the jagirdari crisis as settled fact.
