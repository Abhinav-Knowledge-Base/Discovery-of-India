## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Sacred buildings are ritual centers, employers, landholders, political claims, and contested archives—not timeless symbols alone. |
| **Years** | c. 1000–1700 |
| **Before–after** | From regional sacred landscapes to layered early-modern institutions and memories. |
## Before
- Temples, monasteries, mosques, graves, and local shrines occupy existing settlements.
- Patronage and destruction both predate the period and take varied forms.
## The turn
- Expanding states use construction, donation, reuse, seizure, and repair to announce power.
- Craftspeople translate forms across commissions without erasing patron intent.
## After
- Buildings accumulate additions, custodians, legal claims, and community memories.
- Colonial archaeology later freezes some living sites into monuments.
## Act I — Institution, not silhouette
- Follow land, water, food, wages, festivals, teaching, and lodging.
- Sacred sites redistribute resources while reproducing rank.
- Architecture must be connected to service communities.
## Act II — Patronage and legitimacy
- Kings fund sites beyond narrow personal devotion for political reasons.
- Merchants, women, soldiers, and guilds also donate.
- Inscriptions praise donors and rarely inventory those displaced.
## Act III — Spolia and craft
- Reused pillars may follow conquest, availability, appropriation, or rebuilding.
- Each site needs archaeological and textual context.
- Shared motifs do not prove either harmony or theft by themselves.
## Act IV — Destruction and repair
- Temples and mosques can be attacked as political and sacred institutions.
- Documented iconoclasm must not be denied or generalized to every reign.
- Repair and continued worship reveal agency after violence.
## Act V — Shrines and publics
- Dargahs, samadhis, and local shrines draw overlapping but unequal publics.
- Custodians regulate access, offerings, and narratives.
- Shared attendance coexists with doctrinal dispute.
## Subcontinental note
- No single monument can stand for all Hindu–Muslim relations.
- Medieval sectarian identity must not be collapsed into modern communal nationalism.
## How we know
- Stratigraphy, tool marks, masonry joins, and reused fragments establish building phases.
- Inscriptions and chronicles explain patron claims but may exaggerate.
- Ritual manuals, endowments, oral histories, and court cases reveal institutional life.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim every reused fragment proves iconoclasm.
- Do not deny destruction where converging evidence supports it.
- Do not claim shared style equals social equality.
- Do not present present-day ownership claims as automatic medieval facts.

- Use neutral measured drawings and disclose inaccessible evidence.
