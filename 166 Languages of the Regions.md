## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Courts, shrines, merchants, scribes, and performers turn regional speech into literary authority without creating sealed languages. |
| **Years** | c. 1000–1700 |
| **Before–after** | From multilingual inscriptional worlds to robust vernacular canons beside Sanskrit and Persian. |
## Before
- Sanskrit, Prakrit, Tamil, Arabic, and local speech already serve different institutions.
- Language boundaries are less fixed than modern standardized scripts suggest.
## The turn
- Courts and devotional communities sponsor Kannada, Telugu, Marathi, Bengali, Odia, Assamese, and other texts.
- Persian becomes a major administrative and literary medium across many states.
## After
- Translation, commentary, and bilingual practice create linked regional publics.
- Print-era nationalism later hardens names and boundaries.
## Act I — What is a vernacular?
- Replace a “rise from dialect” story with changing prestige and institutional use.
- Scripts, registers, and genres do not align neatly.
- Scribes routinely move among languages.
## Act II — Courts choose languages
- Telugu flourishes under Kakatiya, Vijayanagara, and nayaka patronage.
- Odia and Assamese courts use literature to articulate regional sovereignty.
- Patronage elevates selected forms while marginalizing others.
## Act III — Devotional publics
- Marathi abhang, Bengali padavali, Kannada vachana, and Tamil hymn enter performance networks.
- Oral and written transmission continually interact.
- Vernacular use does not automatically mean popular or egalitarian access.
## Act IV — Persianate connections
- Persian chronicles, letters, poetry, and administration cross dynastic borders.
- Hindavi, Dakhni, and Rekhta develop in multilingual towns and camps.
- Avoid narrating Urdu and Hindi as fully separated medieval nations.
## Act V — Translation as power
- Epics and scriptures are retold rather than mechanically translated.
- Royal commissions select knowledge and audiences.
- Translation can domesticate difference as well as enable encounter.
## Subcontinental note
- Multilingualism is normal; no language belongs naturally to one religion.
- Linguistic identity is historically real but not identical to later linguistic nationalism.
## How we know
- Colophons, scripts, paper, and manuscript provenance reveal circulation.
- Inscriptions show public language choices but not everyday speech statistics.
- Literary claims about audiences need testing against literacy and performance institutions.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim Sanskrit simply died as vernaculars rose.
- Do not assign Persian only to Muslims.
- Do not map modern language borders backward.
- Do not equate vernacular writing with mass literacy.
