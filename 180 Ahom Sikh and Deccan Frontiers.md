## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Ahom, Sikh, Maratha, and Deccan powers turn imperial frontiers into centers of strategy, institution-building, and alternative sovereignty. |
| **Years** | c. 1600–1708 |
| **Before–after** | From Mughal expansion on several fronts to durable regional military-political formations. |
## Before
- Mughal claims expand farther than routine administration.
- Brahmaputra, Punjab, and Deccan societies possess established states and armed institutions.
## The turn
- Ahom commanders reverse Mughal advance at Saraighat in 1671.
- Sikh Gurus develop militarized community institutions amid Mughal and hill-state conflict.
## After
- Khalsa organization survives Guru Gobind Singh's death.
- Ahom and Maratha states remain major actors after Aurangzeb.
## Act I — Frontier is a viewpoint
- Reject maps that place Delhi at the natural center of every story.
- Rivers, hills, forts, climate, and supply shape imperial limits.
- Local polities generate strategy rather than merely react.
## Act II — Ahom resistance
- Mughal occupation after Mir Jumla's campaign proves costly and temporary.
- Lachit Borphukan organizes river forts, boats, manpower, and intelligence.
- Saraighat is an Ahom-led coalition victory, not one hero's action alone.
## Act III — Sikh transformation
- Guru Hargobind's miri-piri joins temporal and spiritual authority.
- Guru Tegh Bahadur's execution enters Sikh memory through martyrdom and rights of conscience.
- Hill rulers, Mughal officials, and Sikh forces form changing relations.
## Act IV — The Khalsa
- Guru Gobind Singh inaugurates Khalsa discipline in 1699.
- Initiation, shared names, weapons, and rahit create a new corporate form.
- Caste challenge is substantial but social practice remains contested.
## Act V — Deccan entanglements
- Maratha resistance disperses Mughal armies and fiscal resources.
- Guru Gobind Singh's final years at Nanded connect Punjab to Deccan politics.
- Bahadur Shah's succession opens opportunities without ending empire overnight.
## Subcontinental note
- Ahom, Sikh, and Maratha histories have autonomous institutions and archives.
- “Frontier” describes contested imperial reach, not cultural backwardness.
## How we know
- Ahom buranjis, Sikh scripture and rahit texts, Persian chronicles, hukamnamas, and Marathi records disagree.
- River archaeology, forts, weapons, letters, and landscape reconstruct logistics.
- Later heroic nationalism must be separated from early evidence without erasing agency.
## Do not claim
- Do not make Lachit the sole cause of Saraighat.
- Do not claim Sikh militarization began from one event alone.
- Do not portray frontier societies as stateless obstacles.
- Do not treat Mughal setback as immediate imperial disappearance.
