## Before

A regulated mixed economy faced fiscal and balance-of-payments crisis amid earlier experiments with deregulation and global integration.

## The turn

The 1991 government devalued, reduced licensing, changed trade and investment rules, and negotiated stabilization with international institutions.

## After

IT, telecom, finance, construction, and migration transformed cities and aspirations, but agrarian distress, precarious labor, and spatial inequality deepened.

## Act I - Crisis and choice

Foreign-exchange scarcity constrained policy, yet reform design and sequencing remained political choices debated within institutions.

## Act II - New sectors

Software services, outsourcing, telecom, automobiles, and finance created jobs, revenues, and globally connected business regions.

## Act III - Informal majority

Street vendors, domestic workers, construction labor, waste pickers, and small firms sustained urban economies without secure rights.

## Act IV - Migration and city-making

Rural, interstate, and circular migrants built neighborhoods while facing language politics, eviction, and weak services.

## Act V - Growth and distribution

Poverty measures improved on several series, but wealth, regional gaps, farmer distress, pollution, and access remained contested.

## Subcontinental note

Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Mumbai, Delhi, Gurugram, Surat, Kerala, and smaller towns followed different models; Northeast and central Indian migration need visibility.

## How we know

Budgets, RBI and NSS data, firm records, satellite images, labor surveys, municipal files, environmental measures, oral histories, and journalism test competing claims.

## Do not claim

- Reform began without any earlier policy change.
- Liberalization alone explains all growth or inequality.
- Migrants are temporary outsiders to cities.
- GDP measures welfare, ecology, and unpaid work completely.
