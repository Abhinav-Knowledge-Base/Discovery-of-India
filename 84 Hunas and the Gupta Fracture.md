# Ep 84 — Hunas and the Gupta Fracture

[Series README](README.md)

## Treatment
| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Huna invasions meet an already changing Gupta order, producing regional realignments rather than one barbarian blow that ends a civilization. |
| **Years** | c. 450–550 CE |
| **Before–after** | Later Gupta strain → Huna rule in parts of the northwest and multiplying regional powers |

## Before
Gupta authority varied by region as succession, fiscal demands, local elites, trade shifts, and frontier politics changed.

## The turn
Alchon Huna rulers cross mountain and river corridors, issue coins, fight Gupta and regional powers, and establish authority under Toramana and Mihirakula.

## After
Huna power fragments; Aulikara, later Gupta, Maukhari, Maitraka, and other courts compete over altered political landscapes.

## Act I — Faces on coins
Use transformed coin portraits and tamghas to connect the Hunas to Central Asian histories, not a faceless invasion horde.

## Act II — An empire already plural
Question decline narratives by separating court contraction from local agrarian, religious, and commercial continuities.

## Act III — Toramana’s claims
Eran and other records show conquest and local accommodation, though they cannot map stable control year by year.

## Act IV — Mihirakula remembered
Compare inscriptions, Xuanzang, and later traditions whose hostile portraits differ in date and agenda.

## Act V — Fracture makes regions
Follow new courts and landholding arrangements while keeping violence, forest zones, caste, and gendered vulnerability visible.

## Subcontinental note
The Huna story joins Afghanistan, Pakistan, Kashmir, northwest India, and Central Asia through connected routes without making modern borders meaningless.

## How we know
Coin hoards, inscriptions at Eran and Gwalior, seals, archaeology, and later Chinese and South Asian narratives offer conflicting chronologies.

## Do not claim
- Do not blame Gupta political change solely on Huna invasions.
- Do not use “barbarian” as an analytical ethnic category.
- Do not equate coin distribution with fixed imperial borders.
- Do not repeat Mihirakula atrocity stories without source criticism.
- Do not call the sixth century a universal cultural collapse.

## Figures, sites, and works
| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Toramana | Alchon Huna ruler attested in South Asian inscriptions |
| Mihirakula | Successor remembered differently across sources |
| Skandagupta | Gupta ruler whose records frame frontier victories |
| Eran boar inscription | Evidence for Toramana’s sovereignty claim |
| Gwalior inscription | Mihirakula-era record and solar temple reference |
| Sondhni pillars | Yasodharman’s victory claim and regional resurgence |

## Locations and visual evidence
Film passes, coin hoards, Eran, Gwalior, and Sondhni; animate dated attestations rather than one expanding-and-contracting polygon.
