## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Women use devotion to speak through marriage, labor, renunciation, and embodiment while institutions preserve and constrain their voices. |
| **Years** | c. sixth–seventeenth centuries |
| **Before–after** | From scattered poet-saint voices to gendered canons and living performance lineages. |
## Before
- Patriarchal households, courts, temples, and monasteries regulate literacy and mobility.
- Women participate as patrons, workers, performers, nuns, wives, and renouncers.
## The turn
- Andal, Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Akka Mahadevi, Lal Ded, Janabai, and Mirabai claim devotional authority.
- Their poems transform body, labor, desire, and kinship into argument.
## After
- Male editors and hagiographers canonize some women while excluding many.
- Modern reform, feminist, caste, and nationalist readings compete over them.
## Act I — The authored woman
- Attribution is strongest where early textual and institutional evidence converges.
- A female poetic voice does not always prove straightforward female authorship.
- Skepticism must not automatically erase women from history.
## Act II — Household and work
- Janabai's grinding and cleaning make labor a site of divine presence.
- Andal's bridal language enters temple ritual and theological commentary.
- Domestic imagery can critique authority while remaining institutionally framed.
## Act III — Body and renunciation
- Akka Mahadevi's nakedness confronts shame and marital possession.
- Karaikkal Ammaiyar rejects normative beauty through a transformed body.
- Hagiography often makes exceptional sanctity the price of female autonomy.
## Act IV — Voice across boundaries
- Lal Ded's sayings circulate among Kashmiri communities in variant forms.
- Mirabai challenges dynastic expectations in songs of Krishna.
- Later communal ownership should not overwrite layered reception.
## Act V — Institutions and silence
- Temples, mathas, courts, families, and performers determine survival.
- Caste status changes whose defiance is celebrated or ignored.
- Seek unnamed women sustaining ritual, food, copying, and song.
## Subcontinental note
- “Women in bhakti” is not one experience across region, caste, religion, or century.
- Devotional agency coexists with violence, exclusion, and dependency.
## How we know
- Manuscript date, linguistic layer, attribution history, and performance context all matter.
- Inscriptions identify women donors more often than women composers.
- Hagiography reveals gender ideals as well as uncertain biography.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim devotion liberated all women.
- Do not claim every first-person female poem is autobiographical.
- Do not detach gender from caste and patronage.
- Do not turn exceptional saints into proof of ordinary equality.

## Locations and visual evidence
- Film women's performance, work, manuscripts, donor inscriptions, temples, shrines, and contested memorial sites.
- Avoid reenactments that eroticize renunciation or reproduce caste labor as spectacle.
