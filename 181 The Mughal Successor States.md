## Before

Provincial governors, bankers, military households, and zamindars already exercised negotiated authority within the Mughal order.

## The turn

Fiscal and military power shifted toward Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad, and other courts without one moment of imperial collapse.

## After

Regional state-building widened political choice while war and revenue pressure opened opportunities for armed companies.

## Act I - Inheritance, not vacuum

Successor rulers claimed Mughal titles, law, coinage, and administrative expertise.

## Act II - Revenue and credit

Bankers, revenue farmers, and landed magnates financed armies and linked courts to towns.

## Act III - Mobile service elites

Scribes, soldiers, artisans, and performers moved among Delhi and regional capitals.

## Act IV - Contested countryside

Peasants and zamindars bargained, resisted, or paid for expanding military-fiscal states.

## Act V - A connected crisis

Marathas, Afghans, and European companies entered an arena made by Indian rivalries and alliances.

## Subcontinental note

Punjab, Rajasthan, the Deccan, Bengal, and the Gangetic plain followed different paths while remaining connected through credit, service, pilgrimage, and war.

## How we know

Farmans, revenue papers, coins, chronicles, architecture, and merchant records reveal continuity alongside redistributed power.

## Do not claim

- Mughal authority vanished everywhere in 1707.
- The eighteenth century was only an age of anarchy.
- Successor states were proto-national kingdoms.
- European conquest followed automatically from Mughal decline.
