## Treatment
| Field | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Logline** | Independent Bengal courts turn river, rice, textiles, and frontier patronage into a powerful regional sultanate. |
| **Years** | c. 1338–1576 |
| **Before–after** | From Delhi's breakaway provinces to Mughal conquest of an enduring delta society. |
## Before
- Bengal's shifting rivers sustain old towns, agrarian zones, and regional dynasties.
- Delhi governors struggle to command distance, monsoon, and local landed power.
## The turn
- Shams al-Din Ilyas Shah unifies major Bengal centers under one sovereignty.
- Pandua and later Gaur monumentalize independent kingship.
## After
- Husain Shahi rule sponsors multilingual court and temple networks.
- Mughal victory at Rajmahal ends the dynasty, not delta autonomy.
## Act I — A river kingdom
- Let waterways, boats, embankments, and seasonal movement organize the map.
- Ilyas Shah's claims exceed direct administration in many frontier regions.
- Coin mints reveal changing centers of authority.
## Act II — Capitals in brick
- Adina Mosque adapts imperial mosque form to Bengal's materials and ambitions.
- Terracotta, reused stone, and curved roofs emerge from craft and climate.
- Monumental patronage can coexist with temple damage and political violence.
## Act III — Court and countryside
- Sultans negotiate with zamindars, soldiers, Sufis, Brahmans, and merchants.
- Forest clearance and rice expansion vary by locality and century.
- Religious change is gradual and cannot be reduced to sword or patronage alone.
## Act IV — Languages and borders
- Bengali literary patronage includes translations and Vaishnava production.
- Chittagong links Arakan, the Bay of Bengal, and inland routes.
- Odisha, Kamata, Tripura, and Jaunpur remain active rivals.
## Act V — Afghan and Mughal contests
- Sher Shah's career reveals Bengal within wider Afghan politics.
- Karrani rulers retain power after the Husain Shahis.
- Mughal conquest meets riverine resistance and imperfect provincial reach.
## Subcontinental note
- Bengal faces the Bay and eastern highlands as strongly as it faces Delhi.
- Muslim rulers and populations were internally diverse; later communal categories do not map backward.
## How we know
- Coins and inscriptions provide dates and titulature but not social consensus.
- Bengali texts, Persian chronicles, mosques, temples, and settlement archaeology must be read together.
- River migration complicates identifying medieval sites and borders.
## Do not claim
- Do not claim Islamization followed one cause or timetable.
- Do not claim Bengal was culturally isolated from North India.
- Do not claim architectural reuse always proves one motive.
- Do not claim Mughal annexation meant immediate uniform control.
