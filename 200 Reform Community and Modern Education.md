## Before

Pathshalas, madrasas, gurukuls, monastic learning, artisanal training, and court scholarship formed a diverse educational field.

## The turn

Missionaries, colonial policy, print, associations, and Indian reformers expanded institutions while debating gender, caste, scripture, and community.

## After

Universities and reform networks widened opportunity unevenly, produced new elites, and sharpened arguments over representation and social power.

## Act I - Colonial education debates

Orientalist and Anglicist policies both served governance while Indian students pursued their own ends.

## Act II - Reform and paternalism

Campaigns over sati, widow remarriage, age of consent, and domesticity could aid women yet speak over them.

## Act III - Muslim educational projects

Aligarh, Deoband, Nadwa, and regional schools offered distinct responses to colonial rule.

## Act IV - Women's institutions

Pandita Ramabai, Begums, missionaries, and local teachers built spaces constrained by class and caste.

## Act V - Print and community

Newspapers and voluntary societies created debate while standardizing religious and linguistic identities.

## Subcontinental note

Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Punjab, Kerala, and north India followed different institutional routes; Urdu, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Marathi, and English publics overlapped.

## How we know

School surveys, textbooks, periodicals, autobiographies, student registers, legislative debates, buildings, and women's writing reveal intention and reception.

## Do not claim

- Reformers spoke for entire communities.
- Colonial education simply liberated Indians.
- Women's reform equals women's agency.
- Modern education displaced all older learning.
