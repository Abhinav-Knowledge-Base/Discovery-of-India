# Production guide

## Governing principle

The image must never claim more than the evidence. Every sequence should make clear whether viewers are seeing a surviving object or place, a scholarly reconstruction, a performed possibility, an inherited memory, or a graphic abstraction.

## Visual grammar

| Layer | Treatment | Required disclosure |
|---|---|---|
| Material evidence | Patient macro, raking light, scale and reverse angle | Object, date/range, material, provenance, repository |
| Landscape | Ground-level route plus restrained aerial | Historical ecology; note major modern alteration |
| Text | Original surface before transliteration and translation | Language, script, date, lacunae and translator |
| Inference | Diagram assembled from evidenced parts | “Reconstruction” plus confidence marker |
| Memory | Present-tense witness or performance | Speaker/community, recording date and relationship |
| Dispute | Same visual basis for serious alternatives | Name interpretation and reason for disagreement |

Use match cuts only to invite comparison, never to assert descent. Establish scale with hands, tools or measured drawings. Do not use generic “exotic India” montage, incense shorthand, anonymous poverty, crowds as texture, or monuments emptied of workers and custodians.

## Archaeology and reconstruction

1. Secure site and object permissions before scheduling.
2. Consult the excavation publication and a current specialist; tourism signage is not authority.
3. Show trenches, stratigraphy, sampling, conservation and uncertainty—not only finished objects.
4. Never touch, move, wet, chalk, clean or relight an object without conservator approval.
5. Do not disclose precise coordinates of vulnerable or unprotected sites.
6. Reconstruct only features supported by excavation, comparanda or experimental archaeology.
7. Give every reconstruction a version number, sources, author and confidence grade.
8. Keep hypothetical colour, height, roof form, clothing and vegetation visually distinguishable.
9. No crowds generated merely to make a settlement look “alive”; population estimates belong in the evidence log.
10. Human remains require institutional approval, descendant/community consultation where applicable, no sensational close-ups and a clear scientific purpose.
11. Ancient DNA graphics show samples and uncertainty, not essentialized modern populations.
12. A replica must be labelled on first appearance and in credits.

## Maps

- Ancient and medieval maps do **not** display present international or state borders.
- Use rivers, watersheds, coasts, passes, ecological zones, settlements, routes and findspots as the base layer.
- Political reach is a graded surface: directly evidenced core, probable influence, claimed/tributary relation and unknown. Avoid hard “empire blobs.”
- Animate change through dated evidence points; do not morph one modern nation into another.
- Border lines appear for the colonial boundary-making episode and after 1947 when historically relevant.
- Disputed contemporary boundaries use the production’s legally reviewed neutral convention and an explanatory note; a map is not an adjudication.
- Place-name labels carry date and language context. Modern locator appears second and smaller.
- Sea routes are corridors, not precise tracks unless a log supports them.
- Every map stores sources, projection, date frame, cartographer, uncertainty and clearance status.

## Manuscripts, inscriptions and artifacts

Film the carrier before extracting “content”: stone face, copper plate, palm leaf, birch bark, paper, seal, coin edge, textile weave. Begin with conservation-safe wide shots, then raking/multispectral material supplied or approved by the repository. Translation overlays must align to the readable segment and preserve breaks, damaged text and uncertain readings. Never animate a missing line as certain.

Coins receive obverse, reverse, edge, weight and scale; explain that minting, findspot and circulation are different facts. Sculpture is filmed in the round where possible, with original architectural context distinguished from current museum display. Architecture includes quarry, joinery, repair, patronage and labour—not just devotional or imperial meaning.

## Violence, communal history and Partition

- Establish analytical need before showing bodies, wounds, execution, sexual violence or distressed witnesses.
- Warn viewers; avoid looping, slow motion, dramatic zooms and decorative music.
- Do not assign collective guilt to present communities.
- Name perpetrators only at the evidentiary scale available: individual, unit, organization, crowd, authority or unknown.
- Temple, mosque, shrine and icon destruction is dated and contextualized alongside political economy, reuse, patronage and comparable acts; no atrocity montage across centuries.
- Conversion is never assumed from names, architecture or later census labels.
- Partition interviews are voluntary, trauma-informed and interruptible. Do not press for graphic detail.
- Protect locations and identities where testimony may expose risk. Offer transcript review for factual and privacy concerns without surrendering editorial independence.
- Show displacement across Punjab, Bengal, Sindh, Jammu and Kashmir and other affected regions; include caste, gender and class differences.
- Archive footage is not generic: verify date, place and original caption.

## Language and subtitles

The presenter may bridge in English/Hindustani, but local scholars, artisans and communities speak in the language in which they can be most exact. Subtitles preserve register, kin terms, caste/community names and contested concepts. First use may read, for example, “dhamma (ethical-political order; meaning contested),” after which the source word remains.

No fake accents, Sanskritization/Persianization for atmosphere, or modern standardized pronunciation presented as uniquely authentic. Historical dialogue is sparse and consultant-reviewed. On-screen text includes original script, transliteration standard named in notes, idiomatic translation and literal gloss when the distinction matters. Accessibility deliverables include SDH, audio description, speaker IDs, sound cues and readable contrast.

## Presenter structure

1. **Question in place:** presenter states a bounded question, not a verdict.
2. **Encounter:** object/site/craftsperson establishes evidence.
3. **Specialist exchange:** questions expose method and limits; no walk-and-talk exposition dump.
4. **Counter-reading:** a second source or scholar tests the first account.
5. **People and systems:** return institutions to households, labour and ecology.
6. **Evidence board:** what is evidenced, inferred, remembered and unknown.
7. **Hand-off:** identify the change that makes the next episode necessary.

No presenter “discovers” what local residents or scholarship already knows. Credit researchers and translators on screen where their intellectual contribution is visible.

## Sound and music

Record location wild tracks and room tone. Never import jungle, market, call-to-prayer, temple bell or crowd sound from another place as actuality. Reconstructed sound is labelled in notes and based on playable replicas or documented practice. Music does not assign one instrument to one religion or region across centuries. Commissioned score avoids triumphal nationalism, “mystical East” palettes and percussion under violence. Archive recordings retain provenance and rights metadata; silence is preferred to emotional coercion.

## Reenactment rules

- Reenactments stage documented actions or plausible processes, not invented private psychology.
- Camera never imitates archive/news footage; a consistent visual frame marks reconstruction.
- Composite characters are avoided. If unavoidable for process explanation, identify them as composites.
- Dialogue comes only from a cited contemporary source or is labelled dramatized paraphrase.
- Clothing, tools, gesture, food and built setting require a dated research packet.
- Do not darken skin, code villainy by accent, sexualize court life or use caste/religion as costume shorthand.
- Battle scenes prioritize logistics, fear, injury and civilian consequence over spectacle.
- Child performers, animals, fire, weapons and water require enhanced safety review.
- AI-generated people, voices, sites or “archive” are prohibited as evidentiary images. Explanatory synthetic visualization, if approved, is conspicuously labelled.
